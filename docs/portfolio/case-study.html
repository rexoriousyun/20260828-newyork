TTC reliability · UX case study

Which half was true

A brief claimed Toronto's transit delays cluster on specific lanes because of maintenance and infrastructure, and asked for a map of the fastest and most reliable way from A to B. Half of that was right. Finding out which half took precedence over designing anything — and then kept overturning what had already been designed.

01E-D01E-D02

Interrogating the premise before designing for it

Toronto publishes every TTC delay incident since 2014. The load-bearing question was not what to build — it was whether unreliability is a stable property of a place or just noise. If it is noise, there is nothing to predict and no product. I ranked stations independently across two periods and correlated the rankings.

Spearman rho = 0.78. Bad places stay bad. The premise held. The explanation did not.

The brief blamed maintenance and infrastructure. The records say otherwise: injured or ill customer 11.7%, disorderly patron 9.9%, unauthorised at track level 5.2%, security 5.2% — roughly a third of subway delay is passenger and security incidents, with equipment a much thinner slice.

That mattered for design, not just for accuracy. Infrastructure wear is opaque to a rider. Crowding and time of day are legible — things a person can plan around. The product got more useful the moment the real cause was known.

02E-D09P-02

The finding that relocated the whole product

Every public tool reports Min Delay — how late the vehicle is. The same dataset carries Min Gap: the headway hole left behind, which is what the rider actually stands in.

ModeMedian delayMedian gapRatio
Subway5 min8 min1.38×
Bus13 min26 min1.58×

83.8% of bus incidents carry the bunching signature. The industry-standard metric understates bus pain by around 58%, and the correction is sitting unused in public data.

It also settled the target. Subway: 117,924 delay-minutes. Bus: 2,052,791 — about seventeen times as much. The subway gets the attention; the buses carry the pain. I had already recommended a subway-first release. The data killed that recommendation, and the reversal went in the log rather than being quietly corrected.

03E-D03P-04

The trap I nearly shipped

20.7% of subway delay-minutes are attributed to twelve of about seventy stations — and they are terminals and yards. Kipling is not dangerous to ride through. It is where trains turn around and where the log entry gets written.

A naive "avoid the red stations" map would have been confidently wrong, and every competing visualisation I reviewed appears to make exactly this mistake. The same artifact reappeared on the surface network wearing different names — garages and loops, nearly 5% of surface delay logged where no rider is ever waiting.

Cheap to get wrong. Invisible once shipped. This is the finding I am most glad the research surfaced early, because no amount of interface craft recovers from it.

04D-01 … D-35

A system, not a pile of opinions

The client asked for a UX-driven project, so decisions needed to survive being questioned six weeks later. Every claim in this study resolves to an identifier in a chain:

Evidence→Problem→Principle→Decision→Implementation

Four rules make it work. No orphan decisions — every decision cites a principle, every principle cites evidence, and anything without a chain is labelled a preference. Evidence expires, so each claim records when it was measured. Every decision names its kill condition, because one nobody can imagine reversing is usually an unexamined assumption. And superseded, never deleted.

05U-02U-04U-05

Cutting the personas down

The first pass produced five. Three survived.

The Deadline Commuter was not a persona. A hard arrival time changes how a rider reads a number; it does not make them a different rider. The segmentation research divides riders by regularity, not by deadline — and the two descriptions were plainly the same human, since precarious work and unforgiving deadlines travel together in Toronto. Merged as a modifier.

The Regional Commuter had not earned its place. Its evidence described an axis another persona already occupied — having an alternative — without showing a different need. Demoted to a documented hypothesis with a revisit condition, so it does not get silently reinvented.

What remained splits on the one axis the research actually supports: does this rider have an alternative?

06E-D10D-11

The measurement that invalidated my own contract

The product was staked on reliability below the route level. Before building on that, I tested whether it persists at that granularity — and split the question in two.

MetricSplit-half rhoTies
Incident count0.76241%
Total gap-minutes (exposure)0.6817%
Median gap0.36194%
Mean gap (severity)0.1043%
How often a segment costs you time is predictable. How long you wait once it happens is not. 3% ties, so the near-zero correlation is real rather than a compressed scale.

That invalidated an engine contract I had written the same morning, which promised per-segment percentiles as the headline output. Publishing them would have been noise formatted as precision. Severity is now pooled per mode and labelled as pooled; only exposure is segment-specific.

This is the case study's real argument — the measurement changed the design, and the system made that cheap instead of embarrassing.

The last row, in full

The product owner asked about riders travelling alone at night. I proposed a feature on a hunch, pre-registered its threshold in the source, and wrote an audit to test the threshold rather than to confirm the hunch. The audit said the hunch was backwards.

Share of departures on service running every 20 min or worse

night 74.3%
evening 21.3%
midday 19.6%
am peak 14.9%
pm peak 14.2%

1,200,534 weekday departures, each segment-band weighted by the service that runs on it. The median headway behind a departure is exactly 10.0 minutes — which is also the TTC's own frequent-service standard, and therefore a threshold that would have fired on half of all service and meant nothing.

Night is not more likely to go wrong. It is emptier — so the same failure costs half an hour instead of seven minutes. Same feature. Opposite reason. And the reason is what the interface says out loud.
08E-D12P-03D-26

Designing for a product that mostly does not know

The uncomfortable result: 86% of bus segments have too little data to score. Only 3.1% reach high confidence. The principle says absence of data must never look like good news, so the honest rendering of a real bus route is a checkerboard.

I did not soften it. Unknown is drawn as a different kind of thing — dashed, thinned, outside the scale entirely — rather than as a low value at the reassuring end of it.

The grammar, on one route

less waiting more most not enough data

Colour on this app means risk and nothing else. Unknown carries no position on the ramp, because a position is a claim.

The principle nearly failed anyway

A map expression written as coalesce(get(exposure), 0) turns missing into best. For a whole milestone, the unmeasured stretches of a planned trip drew at the green end of the scale — eight of eighteen on one Jane-to-Union itinerary. Valid expression, correct data, invisible to a typechecker and to every test.

A principle is only as good as the last place someone forgot to apply it. Unknown is now a separate layer that cannot sit on the ramp at all.
09P-09D-33

Hiding the machinery without hiding the doubt

The client's principle was that magic is best when it is hidden. Adopted — the answer is the product, and sample sizes, windows, filters and pooling rationale wait behind a quiet why this number. But it needed one line drawn, because two things look identical in a mock and are opposites in the hand.

Hide until askedNever hide
Sample size, observation windowThat a segment has no data
Which filters were appliedThat confidence is low
Why severity is pooled, not per-segmentThat severity is not segment-specific
Persistence statistics, methodologyThat a figure is an estimate, not a promise

Hiding how we know earns trust. Hiding that we do not know spends it — and it is precisely the failure riders have already learned to expect, since official TTC metrics "hide more than they reveal".

One full reversal in how that line is drawn

Four separate conditions — today's disruptions, a route that often does not turn up, thin history, what a step-free constraint cost — each shipped as its own paragraph, and they can all apply at once. Four stacked paragraphs pushed the actual answer off a phone screen. They are now tags that open, under one rule: the label carries the claim, the tap carries only the detail. A tag reads "Little data", never "Details". Anything that cannot be said in three words does not become a tag.

10PR-11 · OUT

What I refused to build

A portfolio usually shows what was made. The clearer signal here is what was declined, since each refusal cost a feature that would have demoed well.

Declined, with the reason on record

  • A safety score. The product owner asked about solo travellers at night, and a delay archive cannot speak to personal safety. I built the measurable thing instead — how long you stand there — and said plainly why the other was not on the table.
  • A buffer recommendation. The rider knows what being late costs them; we do not. The app states the rate and the price and stops.
  • Any claim about shelter. The app says a wait is outside. Whether that stop has a roof is not in a dataset we ingest, so it says nothing about one.
  • Accessibility folded into a score. Elevator failure is binary. It filters the route set before ranking and never contributes a weight.
  • An estimate in place of a gap. Records that cannot be placed stay unplaced. A mis-attributed record is invisible and wrong; an unattributed one is visible.
11E-D22

What the synthetic testers could and could not do

I ran four agents through the app with distinct personas, driving it through a text harness. They found eleven real defects, six in code that was carefully argued and covered by tests — including one that white-screened the entire app on any segment tap, because the map library strips null properties and a strict-inequality guard let undefined through.

They also could not answer a single question in the research plan.

An agent's reaction is training data, not a rider's. An excellent defect-finder; a categorically invalid substitute for the person the product is for.
12verification

The habits that kept catching me

Typechecks and tests passed on visibly broken code three times. What actually worked:

  • Render it and look, at street zoom downtown. The wide view flatters everything, and hid a muddy selection tint and a "green" that read as black.
  • Compute the colour, then look at it. The palette validator checks separation, not whether a colour still reads as the colour it is meant to be.
  • Read the canvas back before believing a screenshot. Headless Chromium composites a stale white band over the map; two independent checks proved the map was drawing correctly and the capture was lying.
  • Print times in the service day. A modulo-24 format turned a window of 03:28–30:35 into "03:28 to 06:35", which read as a gap and got written into a decision as "we have not ingested the night network". All 35 night routes were in the data. The decision was corrected in place rather than deleted.
13D-07D-13

What I would do differently

  • Interview earlier — but not first. Six of the seven questions now blocking validation could only be written once there was a screen to react to. Building first was right; not scheduling interviews in parallel was not, and it is why this study ends with a protocol instead of findings.
  • I let a decision outrun the build. Accessibility was committed to as a hard routing constraint while nothing was implemented and no elevator data was ingested. It is closed now — the planner routes around stations that are not step-free — but it should not have drifted for as long as it did.
  • I designed five user flows without asking the product owner what they wanted. The research was sound and the flows follow from it, but they were derived, not agreed. The sharpest instance: the brief asked twice for A-to-B routing, and I argued it into a forecast on redundancy evidence. The evidence was right; the conclusion was not. Low redundancy is an argument about what a router should say, not a reason to withhold one.
  • I proposed a feature before measuring the thing it was for. The night case above. The audit that corrected it took twenty minutes and should have come first.
14D-08 · open

Where it stands

A working reliability engine over public data with no API keys, a mobile-first trip planner that ranks routes by what actually happens, five reproducible audits with thresholds pre-registered in the source, 171 tests, and a decision record explaining every choice and what would reverse it.

Every success criterion is met except one. Rider validation is outstanding, and it is outstanding because it needs riders — not because it needs more building. The protocol is written, with the verdict condition for each question fixed before any session, the same discipline the audits use.

It also names the trap it has to avoid: the downtown persona is easy to recruit and the captive one is not, so recruiting for convenience would produce a study that validates the rider this product was not built for.

The most valuable output is not the interface. It is knowing which half of the original hypothesis was true — and having the trail to prove it, including the six times I was the one who turned out to be wrong.
